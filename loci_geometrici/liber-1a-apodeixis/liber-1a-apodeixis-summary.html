Liber I.A — Demonstrative Science, Axioms & Intellectual Vision — Structural Synopsis¶

Argumentative Architecture & Bekker Conspectus (Bekker 71a1–79a16)

Architectural Thesis¶

In Analytica Posteriora I.1–13 (71a1–79a16), Aristotle constructs the first rigorous meta-theory of deductive mathematical science (epistēmē). Answering Plato's Meno paradox (71a29; Meno 80d), Aristotle demonstrates that all intellectual teaching proceeds from pre-existent knowledge of two irreducible kinds—comprehension of meaning (ti sēmainei) and grasp of existence (hoti estin)—and classifies the indemonstrable starting points of geometry into definitions, hypotheses, postulates, and common axioms.

Logical & Noetic Progression¶

I.1–3 (71a1–73a20) — Pre-existent Knowledge, Apodeixis & Anti-Circularity

Establishes that scientific demonstration requires premisses that are true, primary, immediate, better known by nature, prior to, and causes of the conclusion; refutes both sceptical infinite regress and circular proof.

I.4–6 (73a21–75a37) — Essential (Kath’ Hauto) & Commensurate Universal (Katholou)

Defines the three criteria of commensurate universality: holding of all (kata pantos), essentially (kath' hauto), and qua the subject itself (hēi auto). Illustrates failure of commensurate universality when 2R is proved separately of equilateral, isosceles, and scalene triangles rather than triangle qua triangle (74a25–32), and celebrates Eudoxus's general proof of alternating proportion (74a17–25).

I.7–9 (75a38–76a30) — Genus-Specificity & Critique of Bryson’s Quadrature

Formulates the prohibition of genus-crossing (metabasis eis allo genos, 75a38): arithmetic cannot prove properties of continuous geometric magnitudes except where one science is formally subalternated to another (as optics to geometry). Exposes Bryson's circle quadrature (75b40) as sophistical because it appeals to an unmediated non-geometrical generality.

I.10–11 (76a31–77a35) — The Architecture of Mathematical First Principles

Draws the canonical distinction between common axioms (‘equals subtracted from equals leave equals’), existential hypotheses (the unit, points, and lines), definitions (which state essence without asserting existence), and constructive postulates (76b23–34). Defends geometry against Platonic separate Forms (77a5).

I.12–13 (77a36–79a16) — Pseudographema, Intellectual Vision & Subalternation

Distinguishes geometrical fallacies (pseudographēmata) from dialectical sophisms, utters the famous noetic maxim ‘these things are as it were seen by intellectual vision’ (ταῦτα δ’ ἐστὶν οἷον ὁρᾶν τῇ νοήσει, 77b30), and separates knowledge of the bare fact (to hoti) from causal demonstration (to dioti) across optics, harmonics, and astronomy.
