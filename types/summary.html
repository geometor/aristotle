Content of type 'summary'

Liber I.A: Apodeixis, Axioms & Intellectual Vision (Anal. Post. I.1–13) — Summary

In Analytica Posteriora I.1–13 (71a1–79a16), Aristotle constructs the first rigorous meta-theory of deductive mathematical science (epistēmē). Answering Plato's Meno paradox (71a29; Meno 80d), Aristotle demonstrates that all intellectual teaching proceeds from pre-existent knowledge of two irreducible kinds—comprehension of meaning (ti sēmainei) and grasp of existence (hoti estin)—and classifies the indemonstrable starting points of geometry into definitions, hypotheses, postulates, and common axioms.

Liber I.B: Universal Proof, Exactitude & Intuitive Nous (Anal. Post. I.24, 27, 31; II.19) — Summary

In Analytica Posteriora I.24, I.27, I.31, and II.19 (85a–88a, 99b–100b), Aristotle completes the epistemology of demonstrative science by establishing why commensurate universal proof excels particular proof, why arithmetic exceeds geometry in exactitude, why sensory observation (aisthēsis) can never cross the threshold into scientific necessity (87b35), and how induction from sense-perception culminates in nous as the principle of principles.

Liber II.A: Quantity & Geometric Syllogism (Cat. 6; Anal. Prior. I.4, 23–24; II.16) — Summary

Liber II.A gathers Aristotle's foundational logical-ontological texts on quantity and geometric deduction from the Organon: Categories 6 (4b20–6a35) establishing the irreducible bifurcation between discrete and continuous quantity, and Prior Analytics I.4, I.23–24, and II.16 (25b, 40b–41b, 64b–65a) exhibiting syllogistic necessity, the reductio proof of the incommensurability of the diagonal, the pre-Euclidean proof of Elements I.5, and the circularity lurking in pre-Euclidean parallel-line theories.

Liber II.B: Geometric Definitions & Antanairesis (Topics VI.4; VIII.3) — Summary

In Topics VI.4 (141a23–142b19) and VIII.3 (158a31–159a14), Aristotle establishes the canon of scientific definition—insisting that geometric entities must be defined through what is prior and more intelligible by nature rather than through sensible posterior effects—and preserves the priceless pre-Eudoxian definition of geometric proportion through antanairesis (reciprocal subtraction, 158b29–35).

Liber III.A: Mathematical Separation & Potential Infinite (Phys. II.2; III.4–7) — Summary

In Physics II.2 (193b22–194b15) and III.4–7 (202b30–208a4), Aristotle demarcates the exact ontological boundary between physics and geometry—showing how the mathematician legitimately separates surfaces, volumes, lengths, and points in thought from sensible motion without incurring falsity (193b31–35)—and constructs his revolutionary theory of the potential infinite (dynamei apeiron), proving that geometers require no actual infinite line (207b27–34).

Liber III.B: Place, Continuum, Zeno & Threefold Dimension (Phys. IV.1–5; VI.1–2, 9; De Caelo I.1) — Summary

Liber III.B unites the crowning physical-geometrical analyses of spatial order, continuity, and dimensionality: Physics IV.1–5 (208a27–213a11) defining place as the innermost motionless boundary of the containing body, Physics VI.1–2 & VI.9 (231a21–233b32, 239b5–240a18) proving that no continuum can be composed of indivisible points and resolving Zeno's four paradoxes, and De Caelo I.1 (268a1–b10) demonstrating the completeness of three spatial dimensions.

Liber IV: Phantasia, Phantasma & Ta ex Aphaireseos (De Anima III.3–8) — Summary

In De Anima III.3–8 (427a17–432a14), Aristotle unveils the cognitive psychology that makes geometry possible for an embodied intellect: distinguishing imagination from both sensation and judgment, distinguishing receptive and active intellect, explaining how the mind thinks undivided continuous lengths and indivisible boundaries (430b6–20), and establishing the canonical law that the soul never thinks without a phantasm (431a16, 432a8) and thinks mathematicals (ta ex aphaireseōs, 431b12–17) within imaginative forms.

Liber V.A: Unity, Quantity & Theoretical Sciences (Metaph. B.2; Δ.6, 13; E.1) — Summary

Liber V.A gathers the architectonic chapters of Aristotle's Metaphysics on axioms, mathematical objects, unity, quantity, and the classification of the sciences: Book B.2 (996a18–998a19) raising the sharp aporiai of sensible vs. ideal geometry (Protagoras's circle-tangent objection, 998a2–4), Book $\Delta$.6 & 13 (1015b16–1017a6, 1020a7–32) defining the indivisible measure and quantity, and Book E.1 (1025b3–1026a32) establishing the canonical tripartition of theoretical philosophy into physics, mathematics, and theology/first philosophy.

Liber V.B: Intelligible Matter, Diagrammatic Actuality & Beauty (Metaph. Z.10–11; Θ.9; M.1–3) — Summary

Liber V.B crowns the entire Loci Geometrici et Noetici Codex with the three supreme peaks of Aristotelian philosophy of mathematics: Metaphysics Z.10–11 (1034b20–1037a20) introducing intelligible matter (hylē noētē, 1036a9–12), Metaphysics $\Theta$.9 (1051a4–33) revealing that geometrical theorems are discovered by actualizing (energeia, 1051a21–33) latent divisions in construction, and Metaphysics M.1–3 (1076a8–1078b6) articulating the qua-ontology of mathematicals and celebrating mathematical beauty (to kalon: order, symmetry, and definiteness, 1078a31–b6).
