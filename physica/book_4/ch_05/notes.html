Physics IV.5 — Critical Commentary & Notes¶

Historical-Mathematical (Heath) & Scholastic-Noetic (Hoenen) Apparatus · Bekker 212a31–213a11

I. Mathematical-Historical Commentary (Secundum Heath & Euclidem)¶

Heath (Mathematics in Aristotle, pp. 120–139, 159–160; Thirteen Books, Vol. I, pp. 156, 158, 232) marvels at the sheer mathematical rigor of Physics VI.1–2. Aristotle’s proof (232b20–233a17) that any hypothesis of atomic magnitudes or atomic time-quanta collapses under a rational velocity ratio (e.g. $3 : 2$ at 233b19–32, forcing the supposedly indivisible middle time-atom to be bisected!) is a masterpiece of Greek proportion theory.

In resolving Zeno's Dichotomy (233a21–31, 239b11–14), Aristotle identifies the exact fallacy that confused thinkers for two millennia: Zeno illicitly played off the infinity by division of the spatial interval against the finitude in extent of the time, forgetting that the finite time $T$ possesses the exact same potential infinity by division ($\frac{T}{2}, \frac{T}{4}, \frac{T}{8}, \dots$) as the finite segment $L$!

In De Caelo I.1 (268b1–6), which Heath cites directly on Elements XI, Def. 1 (‘A solid is that which has length, breadth, and depth’, Vol. III, p. 260), Aristotle notes that while one passes from length (1D) to surface (2D) and from surface to body (3D), no fourth perpendicular spatial magnitude exists in physical/intuitive extension.

II. Scholastic-Noetic Commentary (Secundum Hoenen, Thomam & Paciolum)¶

In De Noetica Geometriae (Caput IV, n. 7; Caput VI; Appendix, n. 10, pp. 251–255), Hoenen devotes sustained attention to Physics IV.1–5 and VI.1. First, in the Appendix (212b14), Hoenen analyzes Aristotle’s boundary definition of place to show how relational localization (ubi) anchors physical geometry in actual bodies while mathematical geometry abstracts pure positional extension (situs).

Second, Hoenen underscores Physics VI.1 (231a21–b18) as an eternal warning against reducing the geometric continuum to a set-theoretic dust of unextended points! Modern point-set topology models a line segment $[0, 1]$ via a set of real-number coordinates, which is a powerful analytical representation; yet noetically and ontologically, as Aristotle proved, unextended points can never generate extension by juxtaposition (στιγμῶν δ’ αἰεὶ τὸ μεταξὺ γραμμή, 231b9). Continuous extension is primary, and points exist within it as indivisible boundaries and potential divisions.

In De Divina Proportione (Dedication, fols. A1r–A1v; Compendio Cap. V, fols. 3v–4r), Luca Pacioli repeatedly invokes Aristotle’s De Caelo (In Coelo et Mundo) alongside Plato’s Timaeus on the cosmic perfection of solid geometry and the five regular bodies.

III. Locus-by-Locus Philological & Geometrical Notes¶

212a20–21 — Topos as Two-Dimensional Surface Boundary

By defining place as the innermost motionless surface of the surrounding medium (as a boat in a flowing river is in the whole river-basin qua motionless rather than the shifting water), Aristotle avoids an infinite regress of three-dimensional spaces.
